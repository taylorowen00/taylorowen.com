I joined Power & Politics to discuss the federal government's new national AI strategy — what it gets right on adoption, and where it falls short on governance.

The federal government's new national AI strategy promises a world-leading public supercomputer by 2031, a rise in business adoption from 12 to 60 percent by 2034, and close to two hundred billion dollars in economic gains. On the building of AI, it is precise, costed, and in many ways overdue. But on the questions that have made Canadians wary of AI: their jobs, their safety, the quality of the information they encounter, the integrity of their elections, their agency over it, the language goes general. It names goals and defers mechanisms.

None of this means the economic agenda is wrong, or the AI moment any less exciting. It means adoption and governance are not alternatives. People trust a powerful technology only when they can see that their institutions are willing and able to hold its makers to account. That assurance is what the strategy postpones.

The strategy is certainly not blind to this. The document calls trust its north star, and rightly so. Its own evidence shows Canadians near the bottom of comparable countries in their trust of AI, forty-second of forty-seven in one study it cites. It correctly names low trust as the binding constraint on the adoption it wants. Where it falters is in saying how. It is detailed about building AI, and thin on the governance that would make Canadians willing to adopt it.

Take safety. The strategy promises new privacy legislation and a commitment to modernize online safety laws. Both have been talked about for years, and are urgently needed. Those are the right vehicles, but as described they are empty. Nothing shows how AI would be brought inside either, no independent regulator able to compel data, audit systems, and enforce, no duty of care or regulatory obligations on the firms building these products. There is a clear path to bringing chatbots inside a new Online Harms Act, and AI more broadly inside a reformed privacy law. But for now, the message is just trust us. After tabling and abandoning these reforms more than once, that is not enough.

On the integrity of our information ecosystem, the strategy offers a single sentence: a pledge to protect elections and democratic institutions from AI-enabled misinformation and foreign interference. Of course that is a critical goal. The question is how, and the document does not say. It sets no obligation on the platforms that circulate synthetic content, names no authority, makes no enforceable rules. It gestures at watermarking, framed as a capability rather than a requirement, and a campaign to help citizens spot manipulated content themselves. That puts the burden in the wrong place. Asking the public to catch AI-generated falsehoods while asking nothing of the systems producing them mistakes the symptom for the cause.

Hardest of all is democratic legitimacy. The strategy speaks of democracy often, but almost always as something to defend from AI. It says little about governing AI democratically. There is no recourse when an automated decision harms a Canadian, no way to contest the systems that shape access to credit, housing, or benefits. Nothing that brings citizens into the decisions about how AI will shape their lives. Citizens are treated as users to be trained, not a public to be empowered. Trust in the age of AI requires giving people meaningful agency over the development and governance of the systems that are shaping their lives, and on that the document is largely silent.

What is somewhat confounding, is that the government is working on these very measures. Online safety and privacy reforms are already underway. But that work is proceeding on its own track, outside the AI strategy, and the separation sends a message. It casts AI safety and data protection as separate files, run alongside the AI project rather than built into it. That is the lost opportunity. Folding these protections into the strategy itself in a substantive way would sent a strong signal to Canadians that their safety and their rights are not adjacent to Canada's AI ambitions, but a core part of them.

What would it mean to take AI governance as seriously as the building of AI technology? In addition to legislating on online hams and privacy, the strategy's own approach points to another answer. Ottawa is ready to spend heavily on the institutes and companies that research and build AI. It could fund, beside them, an institution devoted to the harder questions of how to govern them. A national AI governance institute would treat governance as a discipline worth investing in, learn alongside the democracies facing the same questions, and build real ways for citizens to shape the rules, not just live under them.

So the strategy is, for now, half written. And therefore falls short in the very proposition it states as imperative. The government is right that adoption demands that Canadians trust AI, but this strategy does not sufficiently show how they will earn it. That is still to come.
